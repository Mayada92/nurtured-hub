# Teaching Children to Feel: Why We Coach Sadness but Control Anger

My four-year-old brother wanted the coloring markers. His almost-ten-year-old brother saw this as a violation of his territory and yanked them away. The younger one burst into tears. I rushed over, scooped up the little one to distract him, and told the older one to use his words instead of his hands.

Problem solved, right? Not really. I had soothed the sadness but ignored the anger. I had managed the behavior but missed the emotion.

This pattern, I learned in my graduate course on Social and Emotional Development at Ball State University, is surprisingly common. Teachers, parents, and caregivers tend to coach children through sadness, labeling it, validating it, helping them process it. But when children display anger? We control it, redirect it, shut it down. We treat sadness as an emotion to understand and anger as a behavior to manage.

Research by Silkenbeumer et al. (2024) found that preschool teachers used emotion coaching significantly more often with sadness than with anger. This matters because anger is one of the hardest emotions for children to regulate, yet we give them the least support in learning how to handle it. What if, instead of just stopping the conflict between my brothers, I helped the older one understand and process his anger? What if I gave the younger one words for his frustration before it escalated to tears?

This realization changed how I think about emotional development. It's not just about stopping bad behavior. It's about teaching children to recognize, understand, and regulate their feelings, especially the difficult ones.

## What Is Emotion Coaching?

According to Silkenbeumer et al. (2024), emotion coaching is a powerful way that promotes emotional regulation, especially with young kids. I like to think of the three types of emotion coaching in terms of what, why, and how. The first type is the what: describing what they feel. This includes labeling and validating the feeling in response to a certain event. The second type is the why: describing the feeling and the root cause of it to help guide them, process the emotion, understand it, and deal with it. The third method is the how: a deeper reflection and guiding them in how to overcome the feeling. This may include why they felt the way they did, how it manifested in them, and how they can use techniques to deal with it. This last type is more suitable for older children or adolescents because it requires language skills and reflection. Personally, I find myself often using the first two types, especially labeling and explaining feelings, when I interact with younger children in my family.

## What Is Emotion Co-Regulation?

The article also discussed emotion co-regulation strategies. There are four main ones. The first is distraction, which is helping kids focus their attention on something else. The second is soothing, which is calming their feelings with supportive words or gestures. The third is reappraisal, which is helping them reframe the problem and see it in another way so they can process it differently. The fourth is behavioral modification, which is correcting behavior in the moment. For example, if two kids are fighting, you might separate them so that the conflict does not continue and the emotions calm down. Out of these, distraction works better for younger children before they have developed linguistic and cognitive skills to benefit from other strategies. Reappraisal works better for older children who can handle more abstract ways of thinking.

## Three Interesting Findings

The main point I found interesting in this study is that emotion coaching supports internalized regulation rather than simple compliance with the adult. I think this is more beneficial in the long term because it teaches children how to regulate and manage their own feelings as they grow older. This idea is supported by other research showing that consistent emotion coaching helps children build resilience and the ability to recover from distress, rather than just following instructions (Morris et al., 2017).

Another finding that stood out to me was that teachers used emotion coaching more often with sadness than with anger. I think this is a missed opportunity, because anger is a difficult emotion and children need tools to learn how to regulate it. Sadness tends to be more socially accepted, while anger is often rejected or controlled. Even in adulthood, people are more supportive when you are sad than when you are angry. Research also shows that parents and caregivers often use more supportive responses when they see sadness or guilt but resort to controlling responses when faced with anger or frustration (Bailes et al., 2023).

The third interesting point was the variation in using distraction as a co-regulation strategy. At first, I thought distraction was a weak method because it does not solve the root problem or teach children to process their feelings. But the study helped me see that distraction is actually appropriate when children are too young to use more advanced strategies. Other research shows that distraction can reduce distress in the moment and is developmentally helpful for toddlers, although as language develops, deeper strategies like labeling and reappraisal become more important (Davis et al., 2016; Lieberman et al., 2007). I think the key is balance: distraction is useful for very young children or very intense emotions, but it should not be the only method used.

## Reflecting on My Own Practice

I am very interested in trying to apply these concepts because I have two younger brothers with a big age gap between me and them, one is four years old and the other is almost ten. The usual problem between them is that the older brother uses his physical ability when he wants something and does not allow his younger brother to use it. For example, if the younger child wants toys or coloring materials, the older one reacts as if the younger brother violated his belongings, and he responds by pulling the object away. The younger one usually reacts with crying immediately.

In these situations, I usually just do behavioral correction. I distract the younger brother, and I advise the older one to use words rather than physical strength. Now I am thinking of dealing with it differently. I want to understand the older brother's anger more, instead of only focusing on the sadness of the younger one. I also want to help the four-year-old not only by distraction but by labeling his emotions to start preparing him for higher levels of regulation. Another area for growth for me is learning to be more understanding of anger, not just sadness. I want to practice validating anger and helping others reflect on it, instead of only dealing with it at face value.
