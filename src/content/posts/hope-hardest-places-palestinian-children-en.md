# Hope in the Hardest Places: What Palestinian Children Teach Us About Resilience

Ninety-nine percent of psychological research comes from WEIRD populations, Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic societies. This means that nearly everything we "know" about child development is based on a tiny, unrepresentative slice of humanity.

For my graduate course in Social and Emotional Development at Ball State University, we were challenged to look beyond this narrow lens. The assignment asked us to explore parenting and child development in non-WEIRD cultures, examining how children grow and thrive in contexts that most Western research ignores.

I chose to focus on Palestinian children, specifically those living under political violence and instability in Gaza, the West Bank, and East Jerusalem. Most research on children in conflict zones focuses exclusively on trauma and psychological damage. And rightfully so, the suffering is real and documented. But I wanted to understand something else: How do some children maintain hope, resilience, and strong social bonds even in the most devastating circumstances? What role do parents, families, and communities play in fostering this strength?

What I discovered challenged many assumptions about what children need to flourish. The research revealed that resilience doesn't emerge despite hardship, it can actually develop through how families and communities frame that hardship, giving it meaning through shared identity, spiritual faith, and collective purpose.

## Study Highlights

I chose to include Harazneh et al. (2021) and Veronese et al. (2025) because they collectively tell a complete story. One used a qualitative approach and the other quantitative. Each has its advantages and disadvantages. That is why I consider that they complement each other.

**Harazneh et al. (2021)**  
A qualitative study that examined how Palestinian children exposed to political detention in the West Bank and Gaza developed resilience through socialization processes. The researchers conducted interviews with children aged 10-13 who had experienced the arrest or detention of a close relative. The study found that children developed early political awareness, strong identity, and emotional coping skills through family rituals, community support, and cultural narratives that framed hardship as part of a shared national struggle.

**Veronese et al. (2025)**  
A quantitative, longitudinal study that analyzed 965 Palestinian children, living in Gaza, the West Bank, and East Jerusalem. Data were collected at two time points (T0 and T1) one year apart. The researchers examined how exposure to political violence, family support, and spirituality predicted children's psychological symptoms and subjective well-being. Despite exposure to trauma, children who reported higher spirituality and stronger perceived family support also reported greater hope and life satisfaction, even in such a distressed and unstable environment.

## Reflection on Resilience and Community

I do understand that Palestinian children are traumatized, distressed, and living in survival mode. However, we have been inspired by how they are hopeful. We see them through videos, witnessing their strength, carrying each other physically and emotionally. They play a part of the support system in the community, rather than being treated as children who don't have an active role. I am inspired by them and curious about what their parents' role in their upbringing. It is not even an exclusive role dependent only on the mother and father. The community as a whole plants a seed of collective identity so strong that it is passed on to the children.

Many papers talked about trauma, but these two included resilience and positive developmental indicators. They acknowledged distress and mental health complications, yet highlighted how these children remain inspirational. This does not come from a vacuum. It comes from parents, community, and culture.

## Parenting Dimensions in Palestinian Context

In Chapter 7 of our textbook (Parke et al., 2019), parenting styles, authoritative, authoritarian, permissive, and uninvolved, are defined by combinations of warmth and control. While these styles can still apply to Palestinian parenting, there are variations influenced by their unique context.

In Harazneh et al. (2021), children described deep emotional connections with their family and community, which indicates warmth. Detained relatives were honored rather than hidden or shamed. These experiences were normalized, which created transparency and emotional safety. Cultural rituals like storytelling and commemorating identity contributed to a nurturing environment. In Veronese et al. (2025), although psychological symptoms were present, children still reported high hope and life satisfaction. These are strong indicators of emotional support and warmth, even in the presence of trauma.

In terms of control, Harazneh's study emphasized spiritual and cultural values, loyalty to the cause, and internalized moral expectations. This shows self-discipline rooted in collective identity. It was not punishment-based control, but value-driven expectations of behavior. The second study showed that children had a sense of agency and self-regulation, suggesting parenting that includes guidance and structure.

Palestinian parenting shows a collective approach. The family, extended family, community, and cultural rituals all shape the child's experience. It is like having many caregivers, all working from shared values. Their belief in God, reward, and purpose beyond this life helps them cope with pain and loss. Religion provides guidance in how to feel, act, and respond to hardship. Children can understand these values when taught gradually. They do have the capacity to connect spiritually, form strong values, and develop a powerful sense of identity.

## Comparison with Mexican-American Parenting

In contrast, I can see how maternal depression and acculturative stress can lead to authoritarian parenting, which is high control and low warmth, especially in the context of Mexican-origin mothers raising their children in the America. Calzada et al. (2019) show that both maternal depression and the psychological stress of navigating a new culture are associated with more authoritarian parenting practices, which in turn predict higher behavioral problems in children. While the study does not explicitly discuss cultural or spiritual identity, from my exposure to several Mexican American friends while living in California, I've noticed that many hold strong cultural and spiritual values. However, they are raising their children in a broader environment where these values may not be fully reflected or affirmed. I believe this can create a conflict for the children, where they may feel caught between two cultural identities, unsure whether they are more Mexican or more American. Compared to that, Palestinian society tends to be more homogeneous. This means that the values taught at home are often reinforced by the surrounding community, which helps align the child's internal and external environments.

Reflecting on all three studies, I've realized that parenting is shaped by culture, context, and community. Even when parents have a strong identity, children can struggle if the environment contradicts it. But when family, faith, and community are aligned, children thrive with a strong, rooted identity.
