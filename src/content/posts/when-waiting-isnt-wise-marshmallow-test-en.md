# When Waiting Isn't Wise: Rethinking the Marshmallow Test

What if the famous marshmallow test doesn't measure what we think it does? For decades, the marshmallow test has been held up as the gold standard for predicting children's future success. The premise is simple: children who can resist eating one marshmallow now to get two marshmallows later demonstrate self-control, and this self-control predicts better life outcomes. But what if a child's decision to eat the marshmallow immediately isn't about poor self-control at all? What if it's actually a smart, adaptive response to their environment?

This question lies at the heart of recent research challenging our assumptions about delayed gratification. In my graduate course on Social and Emotional Development at Ball State University, we examined studies comparing how Japanese and American children approach the marshmallow test, and more critically, we explored research showing that for children from low-income families, choosing immediate gratification sometimes correlates with better school outcomes.

These findings forced me to reconsider what we're really measuring when we test delay of gratification. Are we measuring universal self-control? Or are we measuring culturally-specific habits and context-dependent decision-making? The answer has profound implications for how we understand and teach children.

## Defining Delay of Gratification

Delay of gratification is the ability to perform a task or hold off a task without an instant reward, but with a promise or belief that the reward will occur at a later time. Yanaoka et al. (2022) compared Japanese and American children on their ability to delay gratification. Japanese children in the study performed various techniques to help them withhold from eating the marshmallow early, such as singing or looking away. The American child stared directly at the marshmallow and tried to resist without distraction. That made the task much harder, and more American children than Japanese children did not succeed in delaying gratification.

## Cultural Context: Learning to Wait

Japanese children are taught how to delay gratification and probably develop the skills that can help them build patience and techniques to wait. For example, they may wait until everybody is seated at the table before eating. Yanaoka et al. (2022) explain that this is not necessarily the case with American children, where individuality and uniqueness are emphasized rather than learning patience through shared contexts.

## Challenging the Universal Value: When Immediate Gratification Is Adaptive

Duran and Grissmer (2020) showed that in low-income families of color, children who chose immediate gratification sometimes did better at school. This challenges the traditional idea that delayed gratification always predicts success. What I take from this is that it is not about blindly delaying or taking immediately, but about making the right decision in context. I connected this with conversations I had with Palestinian families under extreme hunger and poverty. They explained that food prices can change drastically, but they do not have the liberty to wait for lower prices because they are in survival mode. They need to buy food whenever they can, even if it is not the perfect timing. In such contexts, immediate gratification is not a sign of weakness but of survival. It shows that the real target is decision-making and timing, not simply whether you delay or not.

## Implications for Practice

I think it depends on the age of the children that I interact with. I am not an educator, but if I imagine myself ideally as one, I would try to tap into their thought process. If they are just being impatient, it will be obvious through their reasoning. If they are at the age where they can explain their choices, I would ask them why they made that decision. For example, if we did a marshmallow-type activity, I would not only measure whether they waited or not, but also ask them to explain why. Some children may not care about having more, so they take one and are satisfied. Others may explain they could not help it, which shows they are challenged in their self-regulation. Others may say they acted quickly because it was the wise choice in that moment. By helping children reflect on their choices, I would be teaching them awareness and decision-making. This is important because it respects different backgrounds and avoids labeling children unfairly, especially those whose immediate choices are actually adaptive in their context.
