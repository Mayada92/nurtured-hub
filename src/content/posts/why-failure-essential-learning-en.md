# Why Failure is Essential for Learning: Metacognition, Transfer, and Critical Thinking

Have you ever noticed how the mistakes you make stick with you longer than your successes? There's something about getting a question wrong on an exam that burns into your memory far more vividly than the ones you got right. Why is that?

It turns out this isn't just a quirk of memory, it's actually a crucial part of how we learn. Failure provides feedback that helps us adjust and improve. Without it, we'd never know what needs fixing. But here's the bigger question: if failure is so essential for learning, why do our schools seem terrified of it?

Today's education system often rewards getting the right answer over understanding the process. We teach to standardized tests, prioritize rote memorization, and leave little room for the messy, uncertain work of actually thinking critically. We say we value creativity and problem-solving, but do our classrooms actually make space for them?

This tension between what we claim to value and what we actually measure has fascinated me as I've moved from engineering into educational psychology. The skills that matter most in life, like adapting to new situations, thinking flexibly about problems, and learning from setbacks, are exactly the ones our current system struggles to teach or assess.

## Metacognition and the Power of Failure

Being metacognitively aware means that a person understands how they acquire new knowledge and learn concepts. Failure is a type of feedback one receives when trying something new or attempting to learn. It is essential to fail occasionally in order to adjust based on the outcome or self-assessment, which is part of metacognitive regulation. Metacognitive regulation occurs when individuals control and adjust their learning approach, and for that to happen, they need to understand what works and what does not. I am not entirely sure what the cause of this is, but I personally tend to remember my mistakes from quizzes and exams more than the questions I got right. I think part of it is the emotional investment in wanting all answers to be correct or the slight surprise that the answer was not what I thought it was. In all cases, it is important for me to go through this experience to ensure that I am improving for the better. Failure is necessary for growth. There is so much we do not know and need to learn, and failure is one effective way to help us move in the right direction, as long as it does not discourage us.

## Transfer of Learning: When Skills Move Between Contexts

I can relate many skills that I learned in sports to life and professional skills. I learned emotional resilience from practicing Muay Thai competitively, and it helped me become more emotionally grounded at work and even in soccer. This is an example of **positive transfer**.

An example of **negative transfer** is learning the directions to my dad's apartment and mistakenly going to his old house after he moved to a new apartment closer to my place. Another example is looking in the wrong direction before crossing the street in another country, like in Bali during vacation, because they drive on the left side rather than the right side, which I am accustomed to.

The person examined the environment and evaluated the situation in an abstract way, considering multiple possibilities instead of being constrained by the expected purpose of the objects. ## Critical Thinking and the Limits of Standardized Testing

Critical thinking is the ability to analyze and evaluate knowledge presented from an objective perspective, relying on evidence to validate or refute it. I think critical thinking is an important life skill to have, and learning it early is essential. Schools should prioritize teaching critical thinking, because the long-term impact of standardized test scores is not as significant as having critical thinking skills. In fact, according to a research paper on the limitations of standardized assessment in critical thinking, over-reliance on standardized tests could lead to poor instructional methods, where schools "teach to the test" instead of encouraging deep learning (Rear, 2019). Moreover, students are less likely to use critical thinking in assessments solely based on multiple-choice questions rather than open-ended answers. Generally speaking, standardized testing can lead to superficial learning and rote memorization. This may discourage the development of critical thinking, as students are rewarded for choosing the right answer rather than focusing on the thought process leading to it, aside from not allowing alternative solutions. Critical thinking also encompasses skills needed for academic success, such as synthesizing arguments, conducting research, and problem-solving, so even if students score well on standardized critical thinking tests, it does not necessarily indicate they can think critically in real-life situations (Rear, 2019).
