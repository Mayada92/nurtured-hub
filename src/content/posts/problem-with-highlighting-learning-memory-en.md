# The Problem with Highlighting: What Actually Works for Learning and Memory

Remember those beautifully color-coded notes from high school? The ones with perfectly highlighted passages and neatly organized summaries? I do. I spent hours making them, and my classmates loved borrowing them for quick study sessions before exams. But here's the uncomfortable truth I learned in my educational psychology coursework: most of what we think of as "studying" doesn't actually work very well.

Highlighting key passages, rereading chapters over and over, flipping through textbook pages the night before a test, these are the techniques most of us grew up using. They feel productive. They look like studying. But research shows they're surprisingly ineffective at helping information stick in our long-term memory.

So what does work? And why do our textbooks and teaching methods still encourage approaches that don't help students actually learn? More importantly, if you struggle to remember people's names at social gatherings or find yourself forgetting important information, what does cognitive science tell us about why that happens and how to fix it?

These questions matter for anyone who wants to learn effectively, whether you're a student preparing for exams, a parent helping your children study, or simply someone who wants to understand why your brain remembers some things effortlessly while others slip away moments after you hear them.

## The Study Method That Worked for Me, But Not for Others

Throughout my school years, I used to summarize the entire course material neatly in a few pages. I used this method for all types of classes, history, math, geography, physics, you name it. For some classes, my summaries will be filled with equations and others bullet points of key events. My classmates liked studying from my summaries, but I didn't use theirs because the process of creating them was how I learned best. I formatted them nicely with color-coded titles and organized bullet points. When I built the content artistically, I was engaging with the material and imagining how the information related to other concepts in the material to make everything organized. While my summaries helped others with short-term recall, they lacked the deeper processing needed for long-term memory retention.

## What Research Says Doesn't Work

From the article, one of the interesting things shared as "What Doesn't Work" was highlighting and rereading (Dunlosky et al., 2013). This caught my attention as I associate studying with flipping through textbook pages reading and highlighting key points. This method is actually considered "low utility", thus ineffective because it is inefficient, does not consistently boost performance, and can distract students from more productive strategies. It aligns with Ormrod's (2020) distinction between rote learning and meaningful learning. Rehearsal may keep information in working memory temporarily but does not ensure long-term retention.

## How Textbooks Could Actually Help Us Learn Better

Textbooks can substantially improve by putting more effort into making information more accessible to the brain. This would improve the effectiveness of the student learning process, which is essentially the purpose of writing and selling textbooks after all. Textbooks typically present complex information through chunks of text, prioritizing quantity over quality and adding unnecessary cognitive load. Intrinsic cognitive load, complexity of the material, can be managed by simplifying language, using metaphors, providing real-life examples, and also asking thought-provoking questions. Extrinsic cognitive load, how the material is presented, could be reduced through better use of diagrams, bullet points, and structured summaries. Textbooks should aim for a balanced layout, avoiding excessive distractions while organizing content effectively. I will also be bold and question whether APA in-text citations improve learning. Footnotes (like in Chicago style) might be a better way to minimize distractions while allowing students to focus on the material.

## Why I Can't Remember Names, and What That Teaches Us About Memory

I have a chronic problem of remembering people's names and faces. When I meet new people, I don't actively make connections between their name, face, and basic information to retrieve later. This relates to what Cunningham et al. (2013) explain about Long-Term Depression (LTD), where unused neural connections weaken, making memories harder to retrieve. I tend to remember names that match those of close friends or family members because my brain automatically creates meaningful connections. This demonstrates how memories need active reinforcement to prevent decay. My husband suggests improving memory through mnemonics and associations, such as linking names to first letters, rhymes, or personal traits. However, this technique becomes challenging in large gatherings where cognitive load is high. With practice and training in memory techniques, these strategies could become more automatic, similar to muscle memory, helping prevent forgetting even in challenging social situations. When it comes to learning new skills or knowledge, there are more effective strategies available than for memorizing names. For example, in math, repeated practice problems help master skills through reinforcement. Physical skills can be developed through training until they become muscle memory. Conversing with fluent speakers can reduce cognitive load and strengthen mastery of the language. These various learning approaches help prevent forgetting by creating stronger neural connections through active engagement and meaningful practice.
