# The Power of the Popular: How One Student Can Change an Entire Classroom

Picture a middle school classroom where the top students deliberately underperform. They slouch in their seats, turn in half-finished work, and roll their eyes when teachers ask questions. The message is clear: trying hard in school isn't cool. Within weeks, their peers begin mirroring this behavior. Academic effort becomes something to hide, not celebrate.

Now imagine a different classroom. Here, the most socially influential students are also the most engaged. They ask thoughtful questions, help their classmates, and take pride in their work. The result? A classroom culture where academic achievement and kindness are admired, where peers compete to be helpful rather than disengaged.

What's the difference between these two classrooms? Peer norm salience.

In my graduate course on Social and Emotional Development at Ball State University, we studied how popular students, whether well-liked or not, become the architects of classroom culture. Their behaviors set the standard. When academic achievement is linked to popularity, it becomes socially rewarded. When bullying is linked to popularity, its usual negative consequences diminish. The research by Dijkstra and Gest (2015) revealed that peer norms don't just influence individual students, they shape entire classroom ecosystems.

This research forced me to rethink what teachers can actually control. You can have the best lesson plans, the most engaging activities, and clear behavioral expectations. But if the social hierarchy rewards the wrong behaviors, your efforts will constantly fight against peer influence. The question becomes: How do you leverage peer norms instead of fighting them?

## Defining Peer Norm Salience

Peer norm salience is about the influence of the adolescents who are considered more popular in school settings because they have more impact on their peers, whether they are liked or not. Because of their social status, their behaviors become models for others. This concept is important in adolescence because peers start to have a stronger role in shaping behavior and values.

The study examined three kinds of peer norm salience: academic achievement, prosocial behavior, and bullying. The authors wanted to see whether being popular was associated with these behaviors and how that shaped classroom culture. In some classrooms, being an academic achiever makes students admired, while in others, it can have a negative effect, and bullying might actually increase popularity (Dijkstra & Gest, 2015).

## Main Findings: When Popularity Shapes Behavior

Peer norms were shown to influence social acceptance because status and popularity can make even negative behaviors seem acceptable. In general, prosocial behavior usually relates to being well liked, and academic achievement can also relate to acceptance, but not always. The figure in the article showed that when academic or prosocial behavior was positively linked to popularity, those behaviors were rewarded socially; students who were high achieving or kind were more accepted. When bullying was linked to popularity, its usual negative social consequences were reduced.

In other words, classroom norms can reinforce or weaken the connection between a behavior and peer acceptance. Social status influences how much certain behaviors are rewarded or discouraged, which shows how powerful peer norm salience is in shaping classroom adjustment (Dijkstra & Gest, 2015).

## A Teacher's Strategy: Shifting Peer Norms

In my scenario, I imagine a middle school classroom where academic effort is not seen as "cool." The students with high social status are disengaged in class, and others lean to copy that behavior. As a teacher, I would focus on shifting the classroom dynamics so that academic improvement becomes something admired and respected.

I would start by targeting those high-status students individually through friendly, one-on-one conversations. I would explain to them that in the real world, whether in college or in the workplace, those who are both kind and competent are the ones who are most respected and successful. I would also remind them that they are role models for others and that their actions have an influence beyond the classroom. My goal would be to help them zoom out and understand the real-world implications of their behavior, encouraging them to take pride in their effort and progress.

At the classroom level, I would make verbal comments or give positive feedback whenever these influential students show any kind of academic engagement, improvement, or collaboration. Publicly recognizing those behaviors helps tie social visibility to positive effort and learning. Over time, this can reshape what it means to be "popular," linking it to responsibility, achievement, and good attitude rather than disengagement.

This approach aligns with the idea behind the KiVa program (Kärnä et al., 2011), which focuses on using influential peers to model positive behaviors and reshape classroom norms. Similar to how KiVa reduces bullying by changing what behaviors are socially rewarded, encouraging high-status students to model academic effort could help make academic success and cooperation more valued by their peers.
