# The Feedback That Changed Everything: When Criticism Becomes a Career Catalyst

Have you ever received feedback that stung at first, but ultimately changed the trajectory of your life? The kind of comment that makes you pause, question your work, but somehow also lights a fire inside you?

Most of us have complicated relationships with feedback. We say we want it, but when it arrives, especially the critical kind, it can feel uncomfortable or even discouraging. Yet paradoxically, the feedback that challenges us most deeply is often the feedback we remember longest and learn from most.

What makes feedback truly effective? Is it the praise that motivates us, or the constructive criticism that pushes us to grow? Can feedback be both encouraging and honest at the same time? And perhaps most importantly, how do we know whether the feedback we're giving, whether as teachers, parents, or colleagues, will help someone improve rather than simply discourage them?

These questions aren't just academic. The way we give and receive feedback shapes how students learn, how employees develop professionally, and how children build confidence and skills. Understanding what makes feedback effective can make the difference between helping someone reach their potential and inadvertently holding them back.

## A Piece of Feedback That Changed My Career

For this task of analyzing feedback given for improvement, I decided to analyze a piece of feedback that was not only memorable to me, but also had a profound effect on my career direction and success. I received that piece of feedback from one of the committee members who approved my final thesis document and attended my thesis defense. The specific feedback on the thesis document appears in the last email, where the professor comments on the quality of the thesis itself, titled "Optimization of Paper Discoloration via Pyrolysis Using Lasers" (Alhashem, 2017).

The thesis for my mechanical engineering master's degree is intended to assess my ability to conduct independent research on a topic in my field and present findings in a formal academic document and a presentation. The thesis investigated a novel printing method that uses laser heating to carbonize paper surfaces instead of traditional ink or toner deposition, with the aim of creating a more environmentally friendly printing technology. I understood the assignment as completing a research study on an innovative printing technique and defending it before a committee. However, as evident in the email exchange, I expressed concerns about the depth of my work compared to what might be expected of a Master's thesis, particularly since I had moved from my original focus area (biodiesel) to this printing-related research, which was outside my primary expertise due to circumstances.

## What the Feedback Included

The feedback given to me was a mix of evaluative and motivational elements. Looking at the specific written feedback on the thesis:

- Evaluative comments such as: "most have done significantly more work," and "I don't think your MS thesis achieved [your potential]," indicating a critical assessment of the depth and rigor of the research.

- Motivational and affirmational comments, such as: "You did a great job answering [the questions]," and "this is another one of your great qualities," which affirmed both my presentation and my character.

- Growth-oriented framing, like: "I had to ask challenging questions and press you because that is my job," showed that the intent was not just to critique but to stretch my capabilities.

## Why the Feedback Was Effective

The feedback was effective because it prompted self-reflection and helped me articulate the rationale behind my decisions, including the change in research direction. I took the feedback in good spirit and used it to affirm my pride in the learning experience, despite limitations in technical depth. The feedback also increased my motivation, especially as the instructor stated he believed in my potential. I later joined his research group which triggered a life-long passion in machine learning and data science that boosted my career performance and direction.

## Where the Feedback Fell Short

However, the feedback had limitations. It was norm-referenced feedback ("I've sat on many MS thesis committees and most have done significantly more work. And this is from individuals that are far less talented and gifted than you") rather than criterion-referenced feedback. Effective feedback should not focus on comparing performance to others. While the feedback was meaningful and encouraging ("You did a great job answering them" and "This is another one of your great qualities"), it could have been more effective with added specificity and forward-looking guidance. According to the checklist (Ormrod, 2020), effective feedback should include detailed comments and specific suggestions for improvement. It mentioned that my work lacked depth, but it didn't specify what aspects needed more development. Although the professor wrote that "I want and expect so much more from an amazing individual like you," he didn't highlight specific strengths to build upon in future work or offer concrete guidance on how to improve. It also should arrive at a time when I can use the feedback, which it didn't but could have (all committee members received a written draft of the thesis document). It was positive in tone and intention ("I believe you are capable of amazing things"), and it did imply that I am an active learner. However, it did not include specific details that support the next steps. When I responded to the feedback, the professor did reflect something he learned about my performance and thinking ("I like how you defended yourself and are content with your efforts"). I definitely learned something from his feedback.
