# When Religious Faith Challenges Developmental Theory: Rethinking Kohlberg's Stages

Joe is fourteen years old. He saved forty dollars from his paper route because his father promised he could go to summer camp if he earned the money himself. But just before camp starts, his father changes his mind. Some friends are going on a fishing trip, and Joe's father is short on cash. He tells Joe to hand over the money he worked for. Should Joe give his father the money, or refuse?

This classic moral dilemma has been used for decades to study how people reason about right and wrong. Psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg developed a framework suggesting that moral reasoning develops through predictable stages, from simple obedience to authority, to following social rules, to eventually reasoning from universal ethical principles.

To explore how people at different ages approach this dilemma, I interviewed two individuals: 32-year-old Khalid and 9-year-old Omar. What emerged from their responses was more complex than any neat stage classification could capture. The 9-year-old's reasoning, in particular, raised a fascinating question: Can Kohlberg's secular, Western-centric model truly account for faith-based moral frameworks? When Omar told me his answer depended on whether Joe is Muslim, he wasn't blindly following authority, he was applying a sophisticated religious value system that doesn't fit neatly into Kohlberg's stages.

This tension points to broader questions in developmental psychology: Are we measuring universal cognitive stages, or imposing one cultural lens onto diverse ways of thinking?

## Two Perspectives on Joe's Dilemma

When I presented Joe's situation to Khalid and Omar, their responses revealed different ways of reasoning about fairness, promises, and family obligations.

**Khalid (age 32):** "Well, the first thing Joe should do is confront his father, he should understand his father's perspective and get more aware of the situation. A word, especially between a father and son or somebody you look up to, is something you should honor. But as a human being, you should also honor it because that's what we base our society on, values and meanings.

It's not like an emergency where the family needs this money to survive, or for work, or to fix a car or house, it's for leisure. Even though the father deserves leisure time, he could have approached his kid better. He should have said 'Son, I don't really usually get time except this, can I...' he should ask for approval even though he's the father.

There are two issues here: First, he did not uphold his word even though he told him these are the constraints for going to camp. Second, he wants to take his earned money. If he said he was going to take the money and give it back later, that makes it slightly better, but still doesn't solve the first issue of going back on his word."

**Omar (age 9):** "I have a question first, is Joe Muslim? If he is Muslim, he should give the money to his father. But if he's not Muslim, he can keep the money for himself. And my explanation is that if he's a Muslim, he has to follow what Allah says, and one of the things He says is that you have to be kind to your parents, and part of it is to listen to what they say.

He should have watched out for that fishing trip and known so he could get prepared for it. If the father asks his boy to give him all the money, this is not fair because he's taking what the son worked for, all this effort for nothing. But if his father had told him from the beginning they would share the money, that would be really fair."

## What Their Responses Reveal About Kohlberg's Framework

Analyzing these responses through Kohlberg's framework reveals different levels of moral reasoning. In Khalid's response, we see conventional reasoning when he emphasizes societal values and keeping promises as fundamental to maintaining social order. His suggestion that even a father should negotiate rather than simply exercise authority shows elements of postconventional reasoning, recognizing the need for communication and mutual respect beyond traditional authority structures.

Young Omar's response presents an interesting complexity. While his reliance on religious guidance might initially appear as preconventional reasoning, it actually reflects a faith-based moral framework. He presented his own thoughts confidently guided by his beliefs, which Kohlberg's framework fails to consider, as morality is not fully dependent on authority, society, laws, and universal rules. However, we can identify conventional reasoning when he discusses fairness in terms of clear agreements and shared expectations between parent and child.

Both participants demonstrate how moral reasoning can operate at multiple levels simultaneously, reinforcing that categorizing moral reasoning based on a single dilemma has limitations. Their responses also highlight how cultural and religious values add depth to moral decision-making beyond Kohlberg's traditional framework.

## Morality vs Social Norms

Morality and social norms are fundamentally distinct. Universally, morality revolves around justice, human rights, freedom, and even protecting from harm in its many forms. Social norms are both spoken and unspoken rules pertaining to specific cultures. It can include how youth interact with elders, proper meal time, or how to dress appropriately.

## Education and Moral Development

A meta-analysis by Ketefian (2001) examined 12 quantitative studies on the relationship between educational level and moral reasoning/ethical practice. It found a significant positive correlation (d = .44, p = .000). The systematic review provides strong evidence that higher levels of education are associated with more advanced moral development and reasoning capabilities.

## Gender and Moral Reasoning

Based on my limited life experience, I have not seen gender as a significant differentiating factor in moral reasoning. I am prone to availability bias to what I have seen professionally and socially. However, this observation is supported by research across different settings: medical care (Norberg & Uden, 1995), higher education (Bruess & Pearson, 2002), and cross-cultural contexts (Al-Ansari, 2002), which consistently shows no significant gender differences in moral reasoning capabilities.
